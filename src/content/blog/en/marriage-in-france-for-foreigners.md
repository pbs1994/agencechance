Love knows no borders — but French administration, alas, knows them very well. If you are planning a **marriage in France** and one (or both) of you is a citizen of another country, you can expect not only romance but a very concrete journey through the town hall, consulates, translations and stamps. The good news: the journey is entirely manageable. Thousands of international couples marry in France every year, and with proper preparation the procedure is predictable and clear.

This article is written above all for foreigners who want to make their marriage official in France — whether both partners are foreign nationals or only one of you is not a French citizen.

We will go through it step by step: who can legally marry in France, when residence is required and what the exceptions are, which documents to gather, what an apostille, a certificat de coutume and a certificat de célibat are, how the publication of banns works, whether you need an interpreter at the ceremony, how long it all takes and what it costs — and, most important for our team at Agence Chance, how to combine the official registration with the beautiful symbolic ceremony of your dreams.

## Who can marry in France

Let’s start with the essentials: **marriage in France** is open to everyone, regardless of nationality. French law (articles 63 and 144–164 of the Civil Code) does not require either spouse to be French. Two foreigners can marry, a French national and a foreigner, two foreign women — any combination, including same-sex couples, since same-sex marriage has been legal in France since 2013.

But “open to everyone” does not mean “without conditions”. To marry, the future spouses must meet several substantive requirements:

-   **Age.** Both spouses must be adults, i.e. at least 18. Exceptions for minors are extremely rare and require special authorisation from the public prosecutor.
-   **No existing marriage.** France recognises monogamy only. If you were married before, you will need proof of divorce or the death certificate of your former spouse.
-   **No close family ties.** Marriage between direct relatives (parents and children) and between siblings is prohibited.
-   **Real and free consent.** Both must marry voluntarily and knowingly. This condition is the basis of the fight against sham marriages.

The foreign spouse’s immigration status is a separate question. The right to marry is considered a fundamental constitutional right in France. In practice, this means that even someone in the country on a tourist visa can legally marry, provided the union is genuine and not a sham (“mariage blanc” or “mariage gris”). An important caveat: marriage does not automatically grant a right of residence, and a tourist visa does not allow you to settle in France long-term. For that, after the wedding you will need to apply separately for a long-stay visa or a residence permit (the “vie privée et familiale” title).

## When residence is required — and the exceptions

One of the most frequent questions from international couples: do you have to live in France to marry there? The answer: yes, at least formally — but less strictly than many fear.

By law, a **marriage in France** takes place in the municipality (town hall) where at least one of the future spouses has their **domicile** or **résidence**. The key requirement: that residence must be established by **at least one month of continuous living there** by the date the banns are published.

The difference between the two notions matters:

-   **Domicile** is your main, permanent home — where you are officially registered.
-   **Résidence** is where a person actually and steadily lives, even if it is not their main home. This is what helps international couples: actually living in a municipality for a month (renting accommodation, for example) is enough to be able to marry there.

**Exceptions and flexibility:**

-   You can choose the town hall not only where one of the spouses lives, but also **where one of the spouses’ parents lives** (father or mother). The law provides for this, and couples use it to marry in their family’s home town.
-   If both spouses live abroad and neither has a residence in France, an official ceremony at a French town hall is not possible. The alternative is to marry abroad (for example at a consulate or under local law) and then have the marriage transcribed into the French civil register. For a turnkey French wedding, this route is less convenient.

Residence is proven with ordinary documents: a lease, electricity or water bills, a bank statement and so on. Each town hall may interpret the list slightly differently, so the first practical step is always the same: contact the specific town hall and check its requirements. The general rules on choosing the municipality and the conditions for marriage are set out on the official [French Ministry of Justice portal](https://www.justice.fr/themes/mariage).

## Documents required to marry in France

Now the most time-consuming part. The list of documents is split into a “basic” set, the same for everyone, and an “additional” set that appears as soon as a foreign nationality is involved.

**Basic file for each spouse:**

-   A valid identity document (passport or national ID card).
-   A recent copy of the birth certificate: for a document issued in France, less than 3 months old; for a document issued abroad, usually less than 6 months old, translated and apostilled or legalised.
-   Proof of domicile or residence (bills, lease).
-   Information about the witnesses: 2 to 4 people, with their full names, occupations, addresses, dates and places of birth, and copies of their ID.

**Additionally, depending on your situation:**

-   If previously married: the divorce decree or the death certificate of the former spouse.
-   If you have a marriage contract signed before a notary: the corresponding certificate.
-   For the foreign spouse: specific documents, covered in the next section.

A foreign birth certificate cannot simply be handed in at the town hall: the French electronic verification system for civil records (COMEDEC) does not work with foreign documents. The town hall therefore checks the apostille or legalisation and the certified translation manually, which lengthens the process. More on this below.

## Apostille, translation, certificat de coutume and certificat de célibat

This is the heart of the “international” part of the file and the main source of delays. Let’s look at each element.

**Apostille or legalisation.** Any foreign official document (above all the birth certificate) must be authenticated for use in France. There are two routes:

-   **Apostille** — a simplified procedure for countries that signed the 1961 Hague Convention. It is a special stamp affixed in the country that issued the document, certifying the authenticity of the signature and seal (but not the content).
-   **Legalisation** — a longer procedure for countries outside the Hague Convention, going through diplomatic channels.

Which route applies depends solely on the country that issued the document.

**Certified translation.** A document in a foreign language must be translated into French by a **sworn translator (traducteur assermenté)** accredited to a French court of appeal. The town hall will not accept a home-made or online translation.

**Certificat de coutume (certificate of custom).** This document confirms that the foreign spouse is free to marry under the laws of their country and sets out the relevant family law of that state. It is issued by the consulate of the country of origin in France or by a lawyer specialised in international law. It is not always required, but its absence can block the file in some municipalities — so check in advance.

**Certificat de célibat (certificate of celibacy).** It confirms that the person is not bound by an existing marriage in their country. It is often issued by the same consulate or embassy. In some countries, the two certificates (coutume and célibat) are combined into a single document.

The key advice here: request the certificat de coutume and certificat de célibat at least 3 months before your intended date. Some consulates take weeks or even months to issue them, and these certificates are the most common cause of missed deadlines.

## Publication of banns: announcing the marriage

Once the file is complete and accepted comes a mandatory step: the **publication of banns (publication des bans)** — an official notice of the forthcoming marriage posted at the town hall.

Key facts:

-   The notice includes the future spouses’ names, occupations and places of residence, as well as the date and place of the ceremony.
-   It is displayed for **at least 10 days**, counted from the day after it is posted. If the banns are published on 1 June, the earliest wedding date is 11 June.
-   Banns are published at the town hall where the ceremony will take place **and** at the town hall of each spouse’s place of residence. If the bride, the groom and the wedding are in three different municipalities, the notice is posted in three town halls.
-   After publication, the marriage may be celebrated within one year.

The purpose is historical: to give anyone aware of an impediment to the marriage the opportunity to raise it.

**Prior interview (audition).** For international couples, there is almost always an additional step: the civil registrar (officier d’état civil) may hold an interview — jointly or separately — to make sure the intention to marry is sincere and real. This measure, introduced by a 2003 law, is applied particularly often to mixed marriages. The interview is not held if it is clearly unnecessary. If the administration suspects a sham marriage, the case may be referred to the public prosecutor, who can suspend the procedure for 15 days to a month (renewable). A complete and honest file is therefore in your own interest.

## An interpreter at the ceremony

If one of the spouses or witnesses does not speak French well enough, you will need an **interpreter** at the town hall ceremony itself. This is not a formality: the registrar must be sure that every participant understands the words spoken and their own consent.

A few practical points:

-   The interpreter should usually be **independent and sworn (traducteur-interprète assermenté)**. It is better not to use a witness or a close relative in this role, to avoid any conflict of interest.
-   Town halls differ in their requirements: some insist on a sworn interpreter, others allow more flexible options. Check in advance.
-   You may need the interpreter not only at the ceremony but also for the prior interview.

This matters especially for Russian-speaking couples: Russian–French sworn interpreters are in demand, and a good one should be booked early. If you are planning a bilingual wedding, think about this early so that both sides, Russian and French, feel part of the celebration.

## How long the procedure takes

Here it is important to distinguish the **legal timeline** from the **real timeline**.

-   **The legal minimum** is the 10 days of banns publication. In theory, if the file were accepted instantly, you could marry in just over a week and a half.
-   **The real timeline** is another story. Most town halls ask for the complete file 1–3 months before the ceremony. On top of that comes the time needed to obtain foreign documents, apostilles, translations, the certificat de coutume, a possible interview and the publication of banns itself.

A realistic estimate for an international couple is **2 to 4 months** for the whole preparation — and more in complex cases (a slow consulate, the prosecutor’s involvement). Our advice is simple: as soon as your wedding date is set, start gathering documents — don’t leave it until “a month before”.

## How much does it cost to marry in France

The good news: the civil ceremony itself is free. Registering a **marriage in France** at the town hall carries no fee — the state does not charge you for marrying you.

Costs arise around preparing the documents:

-   **Certified translations** by a sworn translator — usually a few dozen euros per document, depending on length.
-   **Apostille or legalisation** — cost and lead time depend on the issuing country.
-   **Certificat de coutume** — if issued by a lawyer rather than a consulate, it can be noticeably more expensive.
-   **A sworn interpreter at the ceremony** — paid separately.

The total varies widely depending on nationality and the country your documents come from, but it is usually a few hundred euros for the whole administrative part. That is nothing compared to the cost of the celebration itself — which is where the most enjoyable part of planning begins.

## Official registration or symbolic ceremony: which to choose

Many international couples are surprised to learn that the French civil ceremony at the town hall is short and strictly formal: a few minutes, the reading of articles of the Civil Code, signatures, and you are officially married. It is an important legal act, but it rarely looks like the dream wedding you imagined.

That is why so many couples choose a **two-part scenario**:

1.  **Official registration at the town hall** — the short, legally required part that makes your **marriage in France** lawful.

## Common mistakes international couples make

Experience from many international weddings lets us name the most frequent pitfalls — avoid them and your preparation will be calm.

-   **Starting the paperwork too late.** Consulates and apostilles are slow. Order the certificat de coutume and certificat de célibat 3 months ahead, not 3 weeks.
-   **Underestimating the residence requirement.** Couples sometimes learn about the one-month residence rule only after choosing a town hall. Check this first.
-   **Not checking the specific town hall’s requirements.** Document lists and interpretations vary from one municipality to another. What was accepted for friends in one town may be refused in another.
-   **Bringing untranslated or uncertified documents.** Translations must be made by a sworn translator; an apostille is mandatory for Hague Convention countries.
-   **Forgetting the interpreter.** If a spouse or witness does not speak French, an independent sworn interpreter is required — and must be booked in advance.
-   **Confusing the legal and real timelines.** “10 days of banns” is not the length of the whole procedure. Plan for 2–4 months.
-   **Assuming marriage automatically grants residence.** It does not: status is applied for separately, and the application for the “vie privée et familiale” title is made after the wedding.
-   **Reducing everything to formalities and forgetting the celebration.** The official part is only the foundation. The real wedding begins where the bureaucracy ends.
